PURE DRIVING

Real physics. Arcade fun.

That is the whole design, and the two halves are not fighting each other. Underneath is an honest simulation — weight, grip, torque, drag, four wheels each doing their own job. On top of it is an arcade game: you open a page and you drive.

This is not trying to be Gran Turismo, and it is certainly not trying to be Assetto Corsa. Those are simulators and they are magnificent, and they want a wheel, a seat, a setup screen and an afternoon. VROOM wants a keyboard and four seconds.

What it takes from them is the only part that matters: the car is real. A 300 hp mid-engined coupé should feel like a 300 hp mid-engined coupé — light, eager, rotating on the throttle. A hypercar should feel like a hypercar — too much of everything, arriving too early, daring you to stay in it. Those are different cars, and you should know which one you are in before the first corner is over.

So: a real car, in an arcade world. Nothing to set up before you are allowed to move, no licences to earn, no career to grind. Pick a circuit, pick a car, drive.

About VROOM

I built VROOM because I wanted to drive, and everything I opened wanted something from me first. An account. A season pass. Nine menus and a tutorial before a single corner.

So: open a page, pick a circuit, pick a car, drive. That is the whole product. Nothing to buy, nothing to unlock, nothing to subscribe to, no account. I am not adding any of it later either. The moment you add a store, the game stops being about driving and starts being about the store.

I am 44. I grew up feeding coins into these machines. That is the entire market research behind this project.

The 90s are the target

Top Gear. Daytona. Cruis'n USA. What those got right was not the graphics. It was the distance between wanting to drive and driving. Coin in, four seconds, you are at speed. Nobody sat you down to explain the handling model. You learned it by hitting a wall.

That is the target, and it decided real things. Flat shading and low poly, because a road you can read beats a photoreal one you cannot place your car on. Big dials you read without looking. Picking a circuit starts the race: there is no press any button to continue in between.

What “real physics” means here

Arcade on the outside, honest underneath. This is the part I care most about and the part nobody will ever notice, which is exactly why I did it.

Four wheels on four springs, each doing its own work. Weight moves when you brake, turn or accelerate, and grip moves with it, so the outside front tyre loads up going into a corner and gives up first when you ask too much. Tyres lose grip as they are pushed harder, which is the whole reason a car understeers or steps out instead of just quietly slowing down.

The engine makes torque at some revs and not at others, and you feel the difference between a turbo four waiting for boost and a V12 that just keeps going. Air pushes back harder the faster you go. Grip is measured at each wheel, so putting two of them on the grass costs you exactly what it should, and not a penny less.

Every car is built from the real one and then checked against the real one. The numbers below are read out of the running game — the power off the car's own torque curve, the times off a car actually driven down a flat pad in the simulation. None of them are typed in by me and hoped for. A card cannot promise you something the car will not do.

Read that table sideways and you can see the point of it. The MINI has less power than the Lancia and still beats it to 100, because it is 64 kg lighter. The McLaren gives away a tenth to the Porsche off the line and then goes 100 km/h faster than it, because one of them is geared for a circuit and the other was built to be the fastest thing on a road. Six cars, five different layouts — front, mid and rear engines, driving two wheels or four. They are not the same car with a different number on it, and you are not supposed to drive them the same way.

The Lamborghini is quoted at 770 hp at 8500 rpm and 720 Nm at 6750. Those two numbers only agree if the engine keeps pulling long past its torque peak, so I worked backwards from what it needs up at 8500 and built the engine from there. It makes exactly 770. Nobody asked me to do this.

Feeling the limit

A driving game lives or dies on one thing: whether you know the car is about to let go before it lets go. In a real car that arrives through your hands and the seat of your trousers. Here there is no seat, so it has to come through your eyes and your ears.

  • The tyres start complaining before the limit, not after. Front and rear squeal at different pitches, so you can hear which end left.
  • Skid marks, smoke and squeal all come from one number. Same event, three senses. They start and stop together, so they can never disagree.
  • The body leans further than it strictly should, because the outside front tyre is the one about to quit and you need to watch the weight arrive on it.
  • The camera leans into a slide, so when you are sideways you can still see where you are going. Sideways is not the problem. Sideways and blind is the problem.
  • The engines are real recordings. A V12 fires twice as often as a flat six, so the Lamborghini sits most of a fifth above the Porsche.
  • Lift off at high revs and the exhaust pops and cracks behind you. Real cars do that because unburnt fuel lights in a hot pipe. This one does it because it tells you the throttle is shut, and because it is enormously satisfying.

No ABS. No traction control. No stability control. These cars are as fast as they are unassisted, and the car will spin if you ask too much of it.

Six circuits

All six are invented, and every corner on them was moved by hand until it was fun. There is no generator that can do that part. I know, I tried, and the results drove like a spreadsheet. Real circuits were tried too and given up on for the same reason: you cannot widen a corner on the Nürburgring, because the moment you do it stops being the Nürburgring.

Each one also gets its own hour of the day, which turns out to change a place more than its shape does. The sun's height sets how long the shadows run; how much of the light comes from the sky instead sets how deep they go. Between those two you get dawn, noon and golden hour out of the same renderer.

The grip column used to read 1.00, 1.02, 0.98, 0.96 — four circuits within six per cent of each other, which is four circuits that drive the same. They are spread now. Grünkessel is damp and overcast and has the least grip of any tarmac here; the Mediterranean is hot and has the most. That gap is worth more than another layout would be.

Dirt is my favourite and it works the rest of the game hardest. Three quarters of a lap is spent sliding. On tarmac a slide is a mistake. On gravel it is the technique.

What there is to do

Every circuit is a three-lap race. Cross the line, do three clean laps, and you get a total — which is the thing an endless practice session never gives you: a result. Laps you cut are not counted, so the total is honest.

Then the wall. Beat the total and you get three letters on the high score table, one board per circuit, ten deep, exactly as it worked when the machine ate your coin. The board records the car you brought, because bringing the Lamborghini is a decision and it should have to say so.

Underneath that, the older game is still the real one: every circuit remembers your best lap in every car, and the gap between that lap and the one you are on is what you are actually chasing.

Coming: a championship — all six circuits, one running total — plus more cars, more circuits, and multiplayer. Racing someone is the obvious next thing and the one I want most. It arrives when it is good. Free, like the rest of it.
